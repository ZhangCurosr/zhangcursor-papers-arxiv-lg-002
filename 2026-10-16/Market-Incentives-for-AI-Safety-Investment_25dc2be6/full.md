## Abstract

Despite their practical significance, modern AI systems pose significant societal risks, including toxicity, malicious use, and misinformation. Their mitigation depends not only on technical feasibility but also on the incentives of firms that develop and deploy these systems. We show how profit maximization shapes safety investment in an AI supply chain with an upstream LLM provider and several competing downstream firms. In our game-theoretic model, we derive the market-driven level of safety investment and compare it with the levels that maximize market welfare and industry profit. We find that downstream competition generally induces downstream firms to invest more in safety than the industry profit-optimal level. By contrast, the upstream provider may underinvest relative to these benchmarks because it is not fully compensated for the surplus from safety investments. Finally, we analyze how different regulatory interventions affect the participants’ incentives and profits, and the resulting levels of model safety.

## 1 Introduction

Modern large language models (LLMs) pose significant societal risks, including toxicity, malicious use, and misinformation (Mazeika et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025). As artificial intelligence (AI) systems become increasingly prevalent and economically important, managing these risks has become a central concern in the machine learning (ML) literature. Although technical methods for AI risk management constitute a large and important research direction (Guldimann et al., 2025; Mazeika et al., 2024; Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022), the economic incentives that determine whether such methods are developed and deployed have received relatively less attention.

These incentives, however, shape the behavior of AI ecosystem participants and hence play a central role in AI risk governance. Indeed, understanding economic incentives and the resulting market outcomes is crucial for designing effective AI regulations that balance model safety and usefulness, especially in common supply-chain situations that involve upstream providers and downstream adopter firms (Hopkins et al., 2025). In this context, even well-meaning regulations aimed at improving model safety may backfire, leading to lower levels of safety investment due to divergent incentives among the participants (Laufer et al., 2025).

Contributions In this paper, we theoretically analyze market-driven safety investment in an AI supply chain. We consider a common setting in which one upstream model provider serves an industry with multiple competing downstream firms and demonstrate how profit-maximizing incentives might encourage safety investments by both the provider and the firms. We focus on lower-impact errors that can be described by neural scaling laws, not catastrophic risks. In our model, the firms can access the LLM by paying a fee to the provider. The LLM lowers firms’ production costs but also introduces safety-related costs arising from safety accidents. The risk of these accidents can be reduced through safety investments, e.g., by collecting additional data. We use standard economic models of Cournot competition and Nash-in-Nash bargaining to characterize downstream competition and access fees, and model safety-related costs using neural scaling laws established in ML literature.

Our methodological contributions are two-fold. First, we link the benefits of additional LLM alignment to competition outcomes. This link shows how market characteristics shape investment incentives and how safety investments affect market welfare (a notion of welfare centered on economic outcomes). Second, we suggest an economic- and machine-learning-grounded way to determine fees to the provider. Specifically, this description shows how the provider benefits from safety investments even when the provider does not directly suffer reputational harm from the model’s safety accidents.

In our game-theoretic model, we show that competition characteristics strongly influence equilibrium levels of safety investment. Specifically, we find that downstream competition generally induces downstream firms to invest more in safety than the industry-profit-optimal level. By contrast, the upstream provider may underinvest because it is not fully compensated for the surplus generated by its safety investments. Additionally, we show that upstream and downstream regulations that enforce a minimum level of safety lead to higher safety investment levels. However, strict regulation reduces the participants’ profits, which might slow adoption or cause the model provider to exit the market.

## 2 Related Work

Safety incentives in AI supply chains Our work analyzes an important problem of safety incentives in AI supply chains. Similarly to us, Laufer et al. (2025) analyze safety investments and regulations in a market with one model provider and one firm. However, they use a stylized quadratic-cost model of safety investments and do not directly model downstream competition and bargaining processes, leading to a backfiring effect of weak safety regulation that we do not observe in our work. Additionally, they do not study how competition affects safety investment and how regulation affects market welfare. Jagadeesan et al. (2025) analyze entry into the LLM market, assuming that an provider can enter the market only if its model is sufficiently safe compared to others. While this work also analyzes safety-related incentives, it focuses on barriers to entry rather than on the market incentives for safety investments for incumbent firms.

Economics of AI regulation Our work contributes to a growing literature on AI regulation. There are several important directions in this literature. Laufer et al. (2024) study the investment incentives in AI supply chains, similarly to our work. However, they do not examine regulations, link AI investments to market characteristics, or incorporate consumer utility in their analysis. Another line of work (Clark and Hadfield, 2025; Falco et al., 2021) analyzes the organization of audits of AI systems. Wu et al. (2025); Qiu et al. (2025); Xu et al. (2025) study the effects of AI models’ openness. Hopkins et al. (2025) discuss regulatory challenges in AI supply chains. Taitler and Ben-Porat (2025) study how generative AI affects high-quality data generation. Finally, a growing body of economic work (e.g., Acemoglu and Lensman, 2024; Gans, 2025) examines the optimal rate of AI adoption, given that AI risks are not fully known.

Economics of ML Our work also belongs to a broader literature on the economics of ML, which studies data as a new production asset employed by modern ML systems (Sun et al., 2025). This literature includes data-sharing incentives (Castro Fernandez, 2023), data valuation (Lin et al., 2025), and strategic incentives for data manipulation (Hardt et al., 2016; Karimi et al., 2022). Most closely, our work is related to Tsoy and Konstantinov (2023), who study data-sharing incentives between competing firms. They use the same model of downstream market competition and a similar model of ML impact on production costs, but do not consider investment incentives or AI supply chains.

Economics of information security Our work is also related to the literature on information security (Moore, 2024), as incentives in information safety can be structurally similar to those in information security. In this context, our work is closely related to the work of Gordon and Loeb (2002). Specifically, our parametric form of safety-related costs (Equation (10)) is mathematically identical to one of the parametric forms suggested by Gordon and Loeb (2002) to describe the risk of security breaches, even though the motivation for this parametric form differs materially. This identity makes our Theorem 5.1 similar to some of Gordon and Loeb (2002) ’s results. However, the results of Gordon and Loeb (2002) do not establish the relationship between optimal investments and market characteristics. Additionally, we want to note that our approach to safety-related costs resembles ex post liability in the information security literature (Moore, 2024), while the regulations we study resemble ex ante safety regulation.

AI risk measurement While we use an abstract notion of safety, deploying AI systems requires developing metrics and evaluation protocols to measure AI risk. See Autio et al. (2024); Giudici et al. (2024); Giudici and Kolesnikov (2026) for the overview of risk metrics and risk frameworks.

Industrial organization Our approach to market competition closely follows the standard approaches in industrial organization (Tirole, 1988). Specifically, we use a widely employed model of Cournot competition with quasilinear quadratic utility consumers (Dixit, 1979). Additionally, to model bargaining between the model provider and the firms, we use the notion of Nash-in-Nash bargaining, widely used in applied and theoretical economic work (Collard-Wexler et al., 2019).

## 3 Framework

In our work, we introduce a game-theoretic framework that describes safety investments in AI supply chains. We focus on a common practical situation in which a provider offers its foundation model as a service to several competing downstream firms.<sup>1</sup> This model streamlines the firms’ operations but also introduces safety-related costs. These costs arise from safety accidents $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , unqualified advice, toxicity) that lead to reputational losses or regulatory penalties. To reduce these costs and increase profits, both the provider and the firms invest in model safety, e.g., by collecting additional data.

As an example, consider several competing travel agencies that can optimize their operations by introducing an AI chatbot for customer relations. However, the LLM might exhibit unsafe behavior, e.g., giving unqualified legal advice when the user asks about the terms of booking cancellation, leading to reputational losses. Thus, the firms are willing to either train a safer model themselves using their private data or pay the provider for additional safety training on synthetic or public data.

$$

$$
H _ { i } = \mathrm { S a l e s } _ { i } - \mathrm { P r o d u c t i o n C o s t s } _ { i } - \mathrm { S a f e t y R e l a t e d C o s t s } _ { i } - \mathrm { A c c e s s F e e } _ { i } - \mathrm { F i r m S a f e t y I n v e s t m e n t s } _ { i } \cdot \ : ( 2 )
$$

Here, AccessFee is the fee for using the model paid by firm i to the provider, InferenceCosts is the costs for model inference incurred by the provider, ProviderSafetyInvestments is the level of provider’s safety investments, Sales is the sales of firm $i ,$ ProductionCosts is the production costs of firm i, SafetyRelatedCosts is the safety-related costs incurred by firm i, and FirmSafetyInvestments is the level of firm i’s safety investments.

In this framework, we assume only downstream firms incur safety-related costs. This assumption reflects the difficulty of assigning direct responsibility for model behavior to the foundation model provider, given that the provider does not fully control how downstream firms approach additional safety training and model deployment. Hence, first, we assume that the model provider does not incur direct reputational losses. Second, we assume downstream firms settle fines, i.e., the model provider is not liable for safety-related accidents. We discuss an extension of our framework in which the provider becomes liable for safety accidents in Appendix C.1.

Actions Now, we describe the participants’ actions. First, both the provider and firms can invest in safety. These investments might take the form of additional data collection, data generation, additional alignment training, etc. We denote by $n$ and $k _ { i }$ the provider’s and firm i’s investment levels, respectively. Second, each firm and the provider bilaterally bargain over the model access fee, $t _ { i } .$ . Third, each firm makes downstream production decisions, $q _ { i }$

These actions occur in the following order.

1. The provider enters the market and chooses its safety investment level, n.

2. Firms simultaneously bargain with the provider over access fees, $t _ { i }$

3. Each firm chooses its level of additional safety investments, $k _ { i }$

4. The firms compete in the market by making their production decisions, $q _ { i }$

At each stage, the participants maximize their profits, given the outcomes of all previous stages.

## 3.1 Game Stages

We now discuss each game stage in more detail. Consistent with the backward induction logic of game analysis, we discuss each stage in time-reversed order.

Competition Classic competition models describe how each firm’s sales depend on the production decisions of all firms and how each firm’s production costs depend on its own production decisions. Additionally, since higher production volume leads to more LLM usage, our framework introduces two new elements that could depend on production decisions: safety-related costs and access fees.

Taken together, the firms optimize the following market profit functions:

$$
\operatorname* { m a x } _ { q _ { i } } { { \cal { T } } _ { i } ^ { m } } : = { \mathrm { S a l e s } _ { i } ( q ) } - { \mathrm { P r o d u c t i o n C o s t s } _ { i } ( q _ { i } ) } - { \mathrm { S a f e t y R e l a t e d C o s t s } _ { i } ( q _ { i } , k _ { i } , n ) } - \operatorname { A c c e s s F e e } _ { i } ( q _ { i } , t _ { i } ) .
$$

Since sales depend on the production decisions of all firms, we assume that the resulting decisions, $\boldsymbol { q } _ { i } ^ { * } ( \boldsymbol { k } , t , n )$ , constitute Nash equilibrium. We denote the resulting market profits by $\pi _ { i } ^ { m , * } ( k , t , n )$

Firms’ investments To choose safety investment level, the firms optimize their profits:

$$
\operatorname* { m a x } _ { k _ { i } } \varPi _ { i } ^ { m , * } ( k , t , n ) - \mathrm { F i r m S a f e t y I n v e s t m e n t s } _ { i } ( k _ { i } ) .\tag{3}
$$

We denote the resulting firms’ profits by $\boldsymbol { \varPi } _ { i } ^ { * } ( t , n )$ , the resulting safety investment level by $k _ { i } ^ { * } ( t , n )$ and the resulting provider’s profits by

$$
\pi _ { p } ^ { * } ( t , n ) = \sum _ { i } { \mathrm { A c c e s s F e e } } _ { i } ( q _ { i } ^ { * } , t _ { i } ) - { \mathrm { I n f e r e n c e C o s t s } } ( q ^ { * } ) - { \mathrm { P r o v i d e r S a f e t y I n v e s t m e n t s } } ( n ) .
$$

Bargaining To determine the access fees, we use the Nash-in-Nash bargaining solution. This concept assumes the provider and each firm negotiate bilaterally over several rounds. At each even round, the provider suggests a fee level. The negotiating firm can accept or refuse this level, thereby limiting the fee size. At each odd round, the firm suggests a fee level, and the provider can either accept or refuse it (see Collard-Wexler et al., 2019, for game-theoretic foundation). This process results in the Nash bargaining solution (Binmore et al., 1986) for each negotiating pair, taking the outcomes of other negotiations as given. The resulting fees should constitute a Nash equilibrium.

Formally, denote $\pmb { t } _ { - i } = ( t _ { 1 } , \dots , t _ { i - 1 } , t _ { i + 1 } , \dots , t _ { m } )$ , a vector of fees without i’s entry. According to the Nash-in-Nash concept, each pair maximizes the following objective:

$$
\operatorname* { m a x } _ { t _ { i } } ( \pi _ { i } ^ { * } ( t , n ) - \pi _ { i , - i } ^ { * } ( t _ { - i } , n ) ) ^ { 1 - \phi } ( \pi _ { p } ^ { * } ( t , n ) - \pi _ { p , - i } ^ { * } ( t _ { - i } , n ) ) ^ { \phi } ,\tag{4}
$$

where $\phi$ is the provider’s bargaining power and $\varPi _ { i , - i } ^ { * }$ and $\varPi _ { p , - i } ^ { * }$ are firm i’s and the provider’s profits if all firms but i have access to the LLM. We denote the resulting fees by $t _ { i } ^ { * } ( n )$

Provider’s investments Finally, the provider optimizes profits to choose its safety investments

$$
\operatorname* { m a x } _ { n } \varPi _ { p } ^ { * } ( t ^ { * } ( n ) , n ) .
$$

We denote the resulting investment level by $n ^ { * }$ .

## 4 Game-Theoretic Model

To obtain analytical results, we focus on a specific instantiation of the framework. In the following sections, we describe the analytic form of each component in greater detail.

## 4.1 Production-Related Elements

This section describes production-related elements: competition model, inference costs, and access fees. We focus on the classical Cournot model of competition of Dixit (1979), similarly to Tsoy and Konstantinov (2023). This model, widely used in economic studies (Choné and Linnemer, 2019), describes a market consisting of m firms, where firm i produces its own good i. In our analysis, we assume that $m \geq 2$ . Each firm maximizes its profits by tailoring its production volume to consumer demand. To formally describe this model, we need to specify demand $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , consumer behavior) and supply (i.e., firm behavior) factors.

Demand There are M representative consumers (i.e., M is the market size), each representing the average behavior of all consumers. Their utility is a quasi-linear quadratic utility function (Choné and Linnemer, 2019), which they maximize given market prices and budget constraint

$$
\operatorname* { m a x } _ { \boldsymbol { q } , \boldsymbol { g } } u ( \boldsymbol { q } , \boldsymbol { g } ) = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { m } q _ { i } - \Big ( \frac { \sum _ { i } q _ { i } ^ { 2 } + \gamma \sum _ { i \neq j } q _ { i } q _ { j } } { 2 } \Big ) + \boldsymbol { g } \quad \mathrm { s . t . } \quad \boldsymbol { q } ^ { \top } \boldsymbol { p } + \boldsymbol { g } \leq \boldsymbol { G } ,\tag{5}
$$

where $q _ { i }$ is the consumed amount of good $i , g$ is the remaining money, $\pmb { p }$ is the vector of prices for each good, G is the budget of the consumer, and $\gamma \in \left( { - { \frac { 1 } { m - 1 } } \overset { \mathbf { \bar { 1 } } } { , } } \right)$ is the substitutability between the firms’ goods. Higher $\gamma$ corresponds to more similar goods and, hence, “more intense” competition.

The first-order conditions for this problem yield m demand equations that link prices and quantities.

Supply Firm i produces $M q _ { i }$ units of good $i ,$ resulting in market profits

$$

Additionally, we assume that the number of safety-related accidents grows linearly with the number of tokens (see Infante, 2025, for a similar idea). These assumptions result in the following costs:

$$

where $c _ { i }$ includes both production and safety-related costs.

## 4.2 Safety-Related Elements

Now, we consider safety-related costs and safety investments. In our work, we focus on safety-related properties that can be improved through additional data collection or generation, along with additional training. These properties include all properties that can be improved through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022), adversarial training (Mazeika et al., 2024; Dékány et al., 2025), or supervised fine-tuning (Bianchi et al., 2024), such as avoiding toxicity, refusing to give unqualified advice, and refusing to assist with misconduct.

Safety-related costs We assume that firms have symmetric costs<sup>3</sup> and that, before LLM, they equal

$$
c _ { i } = a _ { 0 } .\tag{9}
$$

The LLM access decreases production costs but introduces safety-related costs that grow linearly with the number of safety accidents. We assume that the probability of accidents depends polynomially on the number of data points used for safety training, consistent with the learning-theory logic (Tsybakov, 2004) and neural scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022). So, the final costs follow

$$
c _ { i } = a + b / ( n _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { e f f } } ( k _ { i } , n ) ) ^ { \beta } ,\tag{10}
$$

where $n _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ corresponds to the effective number of data points used for safety training of firm $i \ ' s$ model, $\dot { \beta } > 0$ corresponds to learning easiness, a is the sum of production and safety-related costs for a Pareto optimal model, and b corresponds to safety penalty that arises due to generalization error.<sup>4</sup>

In our model, greater safety investments will not decrease the LLM’s utility. The incentive for cost minimization already determines the desired balance between safety and utility and accounts for the alignment tax (Ouyang et al., 2022). Additional data collection only reduces the generalization error in finding the cost-minimizing point and, hence, can only be beneficial for the costs (Sukenik and Lampert, 2025).<sup>5</sup> To practically achieve this balance, one could use a reply buffer of utility-enhancing data or techniques to mitigate alignment tax (Niu et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026).

Investment costs Both the provider and the downstream firms invest in safety. The provider performs upstream training, which benefits all firms. Each firm performs downstream training, which benefits only that firm. We do not assume a specific training pipeline but require that the total pipeline cost, including data collection and training, scales linearly with the amount of training data.

The procedure might be the following. First, the provider aligns the model using generated data. Then, the downstream firms further align the model using their private data, possibly with different training procedures. Our requirement allows each participant to use replay buffers of utility-enhancing data whose sizes scale proportionally with the amount of safety data. The data may be collected through human labeling, RLAIF (Bai et al., 2022), red-teaming (Perez et al., 2022), or other procedures.

We interpret safety investment levels, n and $k _ { i }$ , as the number of points collected for safety training. Since the firms may collect more relevant data for their tasks, we consider their data more effective. Specifically, following the results of Hanneke and Kpotufe (2019, Remark 2), we assume

$$
n _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { e f f } } = k _ { i } + ( n + 1 ) ^ { \rho } ,\tag{11}
$$

where $\rho \in ( 0 , 1 )$ corresponds to the transferability of the provider’s data for the firms’ tasks. We use $n + 1$ instead of n to represent the model’s baseline safety capabilities before additional training. To highlight economic meaning, we often refer to $n _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { e f f } }$ as cumulative level of safety investments. To simplify the notation, we use the following change of variables in the rest of the paper:

$$
n + 1 \mapsto n .
$$

Our assumptions about the training pipeline yield the following investment costs:

$$
\mathrm { P r o v i d e r S a f e t y I n v e s t m e n t s } = \lambda _ { p } ( n - 1 ) , \quad \mathrm { F i r m S a f e t y I n v e s t m e n t s } _ { i } = \lambda k _ { i } .\tag{12}
$$

We allow $\lambda _ { p } \neq \lambda$ since the provider’s and the firms’ training pipelines may differ.

## 4.3 Investment Problems and Outcomes

We can now derive the precise safety investment problems the participants face.

Absence of disruption Equation (3), given Theorem 4.1 and Equation (10), results in analytically intractable expressions. To get an analytical answer, we assume

We additionally assume that the introduction of LLM is beneficial for the production process and profitable for all participants. In particular, this assumption implies that there exists a feasible vector of fees t that makes the left- and right-hand sides in Equation (4) non-negative.

## 6 Effects of Regulation

In this section, we discuss how regulatory actions can impact our outcome metrics. We assume perfect enforcement of regulations. Specifically, we assume that one can implement a properly designed audit procedure (with sufficiently large fines) that corresponds to fixed administrative costs for the regulator and does not change incentives for safety investments.

Downstream auditing A possible regulatory approach might enforce the safety of the resulting models. Assuming that the enforcement will focus on the probability of safety accidents, it will result in constraint $n _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { e f f } } \geq k _ { 0 }$

The regulation does not reduce the participants’ investments. However, the firms incur additional data-collection costs and may refuse to use the LLM under overly strict regulation (see Equation (15) with $k _ { m } \mapsto k _ { 0 } )$ . The effect on industry profit and market welfare is ambiguous. On the one hand, regulation increases firms’ investment, which can reduce these metrics when competition is severe, as shown in Theorem 5.3. On the other hand, regulation also increases the provider’s investment. These opposing effects imply that regulation increases the metrics when $k _ { 0 } \overset { \cdot } { \leq } \operatorname* { m a x } ( k _ { m } ^ { x } , n _ { s } ^ { \rho } )$ , where the metrics either increase by Theorem 5.3 or the firms make zero investments and benefit from higher provider investment. Otherwise, the regulation has a negative effect.

Upstream auditing Another approach is to audit the provider’s model, essentially requiring $n \geq n _ { 0 }$ Proposition 6.2. Under the upstream regulation $n \geq n _ { 0 }$ (where $n _ { 0 } \geq 1 )$ , the provider investment level will be $n ^ { * } = \operatorname* { m a x } ( n _ { 0 } , \operatorname* { m i n } ( n _ { s } , n _ { b } ) )$ and the firms’ response $k ^ { * } = \operatorname* { m a x } ( 0 , k _ { m } - n ^ { \rho } )$ will not change.

As for the industry profit and market welfare, in the case of $k _ { m } ^ { x } \leq k _ { m } ,$ , the regulation is beneficial if $n _ { 0 } \leq n ^ { x }$ by Theorem 5.4. Since $n _ { s } < n _ { s } ^ { \mathrm { o p t } }$ , the moderate regulation is always beneficial when the firms make non-zero investments. Otherwise, when the firms make zero investments, the regulation is beneficial if the market is not overly competitive or the provider’s bargaining power is low.

Indirect regulations The regulator might also increase safety investments through less direct actions. Theorem 5.2 suggests several channels: safety penalty, b, and investment costs, λ and $\lambda _ { p } .$ . By increasing fines for safety accidents, the regulator indirectly increases b (and possibly a, but in this discussion we assume that the increase in a is small). This increase raises cumulative investments (through $k _ { m } \mathrm { o r } n _ { b } )$ . However, as previously, this intervention reduces the participants’ profits. By subsidizing safety investments, the regulator decreases λ or $\lambda _ { p }$ . If the firms make non-zero investments, the subsidy to the firms leads to higher cumulative investments. Otherwise, the subsidy to the provider leads to higher cumulative investments. However, both subsidies require additional funds from the regulator. Also note that the subsidy to the provider might help address the collective action problem identified in Theorem 5.4, allowing the regulator to finance it partially through higher taxes on the participants.

## 7 Conclusion

We analyzed how profit maximization might incentivize safety investments by the participants of the AI supply chain. In our framework, downstream firms are interested in safer models because they incur safety-related costs. At the same time, the provider is interested in safety because it can charge higher access fees to the firms. We characterized the market-driven level of safety investments and compared it with the industry-profit- and market-welfare-optimal ones. This comparison shows that downstream competition generally leads to higher levels of safety investment.

We outlined several ways the regulator could increase investment in safety. Each of these approaches has upsides and downsides. Auditing and fines do not require additional funds and lead to higher safety investments but reduce profits from the LLM and, hence, might slow AI adoption. Subsidies lead to higher investment levels and profits but require additional funds from the regulator.

We hope that our work contributes to AI governance by highlighting the economic challenges associated with AI safety investment for both policymakers and industry.

Limitations While we demonstrate how profit maximization incentivizes safety investment and obtain analytical expressions for its levels, our analysis has several limitations. First, we assume that the firms are symmetric and, hence, have similar production processes. This assumption might not hold in industries with substantial horizontal differentiation among firms. Second, our analysis does not account for catastrophic risks posed by the models. Instead, we focus on lower-impact errors that can be described by neural scaling laws. Third, our results rely on the absence-of-disruption assumption, which might not hold in more dynamic and innovative industries. Fourth, we assume perfect enforcement in our analysis of regulations and assume that one can design a proper audit procedure by imposing sufficiently large fines. Finally, we assume specific functional forms for the elements of our framework. These forms capture the main conceptual properties of the elements but might differ from real-world relationships.

Future directions We foresee several possible extensions of our model. First, a more general model might analyze asymmetric firms, which is important in horizontally differentiated industries. As a related point, it might be interesting to consider cascading effects in which firms drive competitors out of the market by gaining access to the LLM. Another possible extension is a version of welfare that accounts for the harms to consumers from safety accidents. Finally, it might be interesting to consider a more complex provider market, which might include open-weight models or several competing providers, as we discuss in Appendix B.

## B Alternative Provider Structures

This section discusses how to extend our analysis to a market with several foundation model providers, a market where another provider could enter, or a market where firms can use open-weight models.

Many providers We suggest the following approach for an extension to a market with several model providers.

First, we add an additional stage that happens before everything else in which model providers match with downstream firms. Conceptually, during this stage, the model providers pitch their model to the downstream firms and make preliminary agreements with them. A simplest extension of this kind is a random assignment (e.g., the sales department of each firm randomly pass or fail during the negotiations with each downstream firm); more sophisticated versions might assume some model of hidden characteristics of firms and model providers.

Second, we replace the Nash-in-Nash solution concept with the Nash-in-Nash with Threat of Replacement solution concept (Ho and Lee, 2019). This new solution concept changes firms’ outside options. Previously, the outside option was to stop using AI completely. Now, the firm has two outside options: it can either stop using AI or start using a model of a different provider. The fees to the alternative provider should be set so the alternative provider is no worse off when it starts serving the new firm; i.e., the firm’s fee under the alternative deal should equal its “inference costs”.

Third, the extension should also add a factor that makes switching between providers difficult. Without such friction, providers will compete on price until each provider receives zero surplus from serving clients and exits the market. In other words, without such friction, the situation reduces to the previous case with one model provider. Such switching frictions can arise from different factors (Tirole, 1988). In the first stage, firms might make model-specific infrastructure investments that make switching costly. Alternatively, each model provider’s inference capacity may be bounded and, hence, inference costs for a potential additional client may skyrocket, locking them into their original model provider.

Importantly, under this extension, only each firm’s outside option changes. Hence, using the previous notation, only the parameter δ changes. Since δ does not affect the equilibrium and optimal investment levels, the equilibrium and optimal investment levels will stay the same in the new model; only the fees to the provider will decrease. This property suggests that the current analysis should not qualitatively depend on the number of upstream model providers.

Threat of entry A similar effect occurs due to the threat of entry. Assume that a potential alternative provider could enter the market. The potential offer from the entrant will again constitute a better outside option for the downstream firms, leading effectively to lower δ.

Specifically, a potential competing provider will enter the market and offer its services if the firm offers a fee that covers its inference and data-collection costs. Formally, we assume that the entrant announces that it will collect $n _ { e }$ points if it enters the market. Then, we get the following surplus for each firm (i.e., the left-hand part of Equation (14)) that corresponds to the new outside option:

$$
\operatorname* { m i n } _ { k _ { e } } \frac { A b } { ( k _ { e } + n _ { e } ^ { \rho } ) ^ { \beta } } + \lambda k _ { e } + \lambda _ { p } ( n _ { e } - 1 ) - \frac { A b } { ( k _ { i } + n ^ { \rho } ) ^ { \beta } } - \lambda k _ { i } - B ( t _ { i } - w ) ,
$$

where $k _ { e }$ is the potential number of points a firm collects under the alternative deal.

In this case, only a positive externality from the provider’s investments allows for non-zero provider profits. To see this, consider $n _ { e } = n ^ { * } ;$ then, for subscription-based fees, we get the following provider’s profits:

$$
\Pi _ { p } ^ { * } = \frac { m \phi M q ^ { * } } { B } \lambda _ { p } ( n ^ { * } - 1 ) - \lambda _ { p } ( n ^ { * } - 1 ) .
$$

These profits are positive only if $m \phi M q ^ { * } / B > 1$ , i.e., when m is big enough.

Open-weight models The presence of open-weight models will also only change firms’ outside options and hence will not affect investment levels. Formally, if we assume that the open-weight model behaves as the provider’s model, we get the following surplus for each firm:

$$
\operatorname* { m i n } _ { k _ { e } } \frac { A b } { ( k _ { e } + 1 ) ^ { \beta } } + \lambda k _ { e } - \frac { A b } { ( k _ { i } + n ^ { \rho } ) ^ { \beta } } - \lambda k _ { i } - B ( t _ { i } - w ) ,
$$

where $k _ { e }$ is the potential number of points collected for the open-weight model. As in Theorem 5.1, the equilibrium investment level in the open-weight case is $k _ { e } ^ { * } = \operatorname* { m a x } ( k _ { m } - 1 , 0 )$ . This level is generally lower than before because the provider’s investments provide no positive externality.

Similar to safety-related costs, we assume that the direct harm to consumers is proportional to production scale

## D.3 Multi-Objective Costs

This section discusses how to modify the framework to allow optimizing for several properties that follow polynomial scaling laws. Assume we have R properties that influence costs, and that the costs associated with these properties grow linearly with production volume. The results of Sukenik and Lampert (2025) motivate the following structure of marginal cost

$$
c _ { i } = a + \sum _ { r = 1 } ^ { R } \frac { b _ { r } } { ( k _ { r , i } + n _ { r } ^ { \rho _ { r } } ) ^ { \beta _ { r } } } ,\tag{35}
$$

where $r$ is the property, $b _ { r } , \rho _ { r }$ , and $\beta _ { r }$ are scaling constants associated with this property, and $k _ { r , i }$ and $n _ { r }$ are firm i’s and provider’s investments in property r. In this case, we can assume the following version of the absence-of-disruption assumption:

$$
( 2 - \gamma ) ( 1 - a - w ) \gg ( 2 + \gamma ( m - 2 ) ) ( \delta + \sum _ { r } b _ { r } ) .\tag{36}
$$

Since the cost structure is separable across properties and we use a first-order approximation, maximizing profits across investments in all properties reduces to maximizing profits for each property individually. Hence, we get the same results as before for each property individually, and our results will not change qualitatively.

## E.2 Disruption

This section informally discusses possible relaxations of the absence-of-disruption assumption and how the analysis change without this assumption.

Bound on b To relax the bound on $b ,$ one can show that investments will always exceed some minimum level $k _ { \mathrm { m i n } } .$ . Then one needs to upper-bound $b / ( k _ { \mathrm { m i n } } ) ^ { \beta }$ instead of b. To establish a minimum level, one can use a “bootstrap” procedure. First, one considers the “worst case of costs”, $c _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { e f f } } = a + w .$ which incentivizes the safety investments the least, and gets a minimum level of investments in this worst case, $k _ { \mathrm { m i n } , 1 } .$ (To show that the considered case is the worst case, one can compute the second derivatives of $\bar { \cal I } _ { i } ^ { m , * }$ over $\mathbf { \delta } _ { k . \mathrm { ~ \tiny ~ \ / ~ } }$ Using this number, one now bounds the investments from above and gets $k _ { \operatorname* { m a x } , 1 }$ . The argument is then repeated using the bound implied by $k _ { \mathrm { m a x } , 1 }$ and so forth. After sufficiently many iterations, this yields the desired minimum investment level.

Bound on δ An analytic expression is possible in the opposing limiting case, where δ is large, and the company exits the market if it does not adopt the AI technology. In this case, the outside option in the Nash bargaining problem corresponds to zero profit. In this case, the firms’ investment response functions remain the same (since a firm’s surplus still depends on $k _ { i }$ in the same way). However, the model provider’s investment would change.

Allowing disruption Now, we discuss cases in which firms can drive competitors out of the market. First, if it is easy to make certain firms exit by simply adopting the technology (e.g., costs improve significantly with AI for some firms, but the improvement is small for others, making them uncompetitive), then the analysis mirrors our current analysis but with fewer firms. If, on the other hand, it is possible to make some firms exit the market, but it requires investment to exceed a threshold, then firms’ incentives will exhibit a kink (a point where the derivative jumps). Below and above the threshold, the firms’ profits remain smooth and follow our analysis. However, crossing the threshold causes some firms to exit, creating a kink in the marginal value of investment (profits will remain continuous). Hence, the investment levels can 1) continue to follow Theorem 5.1, 2) lie at the kink, or 3) fall to zero if the firm exits. The precise structure of these kinks depends on the asymmetry assumptions and is therefore difficult to predict.
