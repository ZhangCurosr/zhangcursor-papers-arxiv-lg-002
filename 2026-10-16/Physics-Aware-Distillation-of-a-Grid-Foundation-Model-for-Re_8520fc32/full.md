## Abstract

Grid foundation models (GridFMs) are graph transformers pretrained on a masked power-flow objective, and once trained they evaluate power flow orders of magnitude faster than an iterative solver. Their size, however, targets datacentre inference, whereas low-inertia operation — grids with little rotating reserve, where disturbances evolve within milliseconds — increasingly asks for inference at the edge, next to the measurement, at batch size one and within a single synchrophasor (PMU) reporting frame. We distil a 20.1M-parameter, 12-layer GridFM teacher into a 0.62Mparameter, 6-layer student, a 32.5× parameter reduction at half the message-passing depth, using an objective that combines the masked power-flow loss with output, physics-residual, latent-state and attention transfer. The physicsresidual term, which asks the student to reproduce the teacher’s active- and reactive-power residual profile rather than only to minimise its own, has not previously been applied to typed power-grid graphs. We evaluate on a renewable variant of the IEEE 118-bus benchmark grid in which 26 of the conventional (synchronous) generators are replaced by inverter-based renewables with battery flexibility and standardised reactive-power limits (IEEE 1547), giving 97 529 physically valid (AC-OPF-feasible) operating scenarios, and we ablate all eight combinations of the transfer terms, reporting accuracy against ground truth and fidelity to the teacher separately. Distillation lowers student error on all four predicted quantities, by up to 48% on active-power dispatch, and every distilled student is more accurate than its teacher on reactive-power dispatch. The gain is largely carried by the output matching term. The physics-residual term, in the layer-aggregated form used here, is consistently harmful, which we attribute to the spatial averaging it applies to a bus-level residual field. The resulting student is 32.5× smaller in parameter count and has half the message-passing depth, making it a candidate for subsequent edge-device benchmarking.

## 1 Introduction

Power-system operation is moving to the grid edge. Converter-interfaced renewables, batteries and flexible loads have displaced the synchronous machines that once provided inertia, and the dynamics of the resulting low-inertia systems unfold on timescales that leave little room for a round-trip to a control centre [Milano et al., 2018, Dörfler and Groß, 2023]. Visions of autonomous energy grids therefore place monitoring and control decisions locally, next to the distributed resources they act on [Kroposki et al., 2020], and the measurement infrastructure already runs at that pace: synchrophasors report at 10–50 frames per second, i.e. every 20–100 ms [IEEE, 2011]. Edge intelligence, running learned models on embedded hardware at the point of measurement, is the natural enabling paradigm [Zhou et al., 2019, Gooi et al., 2023], but it requires models that fit the device and answer within the frame.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) are the natural learned representation of a grid, with buses, generators and branches as typed nodes and relations [Liao et al., 2022]. Recent work has scaled this idea into grid foundation models (GridFMs): graph transformers pretrained on a masked power-flow (PF) objective, regularised by a Kirchhoff physics loss, across many topologies and operating points [Hamann et al., 2024, Puech et al., 2025]. Once trained, such a model acts as a PF surrogate that is three to four orders of magnitude faster than iterative numerical solution [Puech et al., 2024], which makes it attractive precisely for the high-rate, local inference that edge operation demands.

The obstacle is size. The GridFM teacher used in this work has 12 layers, 8 attention heads and 20 million parameters, and was designed for cloud or HPC inference. An edge device must instead execute it at batch size one, within a bounded latency and memory budget. For GNNs the parameter count is a poor proxy for that cost: irregular gather/scatter and the number of message-passing layers dominate the runtime [Barillaro et al., 2025], so depth, not width, is what has to shrink. The standard compression toolbox, quantization [Jacob et al., 2018, Nagel et al., 2021, Tailor et al., 2021], hardware-aware pruning [Shen et al., 2022] and once-for-all supernets [Cai et al., 2020], reduces arithmetic and storage but is agnostic to physics: nothing in these procedures guarantees that the compressed model still respects nodal power balance.

Knowledge distillation (KD) addresses the functional side of the problem by training a small student to reproduce a large teacher through its outputs, its intermediate representations or its attention maps [Bucila et al., 2006, Ba and Caruana, 2014, Hinton et al., 2015, Romero et al., 2015, Zagoruyko and Komodakis, 2017, Gou et al., 2021], and has been extended to graphs [Tian et al., 2025]. For a physical system, however, a student should inherit more than the teacher’s predictions: it should inherit the teacher’s physical consistency, that is, how its active- and reactivepower residuals are distributed over the network. Classification-style KD has no term for that, and the physics-aware distillation that has recently appeared for PDE solvers and PDE foundation models [Bounja et al., 2025, Musekamp et al., 2026] has not been applied to typed power-grid graphs with bus-type-dependent masking. This paper closes that gap by distilling a pretrained GridFM into a 32.5× smaller, half-depth student with a physics-aware objective, and by measuring the result on edge hardware. Our contributions are:

• A physics-aware distillation objective for grid foundation models that combines output, physics-residual, latent and attention terms with a frozen GridFM teacher (Section 3, Eq. 1). The physics-residual term, which matches the teacher’s active/reactive residual profile rather than only penalising the student’s own residual, is new.

• A renewable-grid benchmark derived from IEEE 118, in which 26 synchronous units are replaced by converterinterfaced generators with battery flexibility and IEEE 1547 reactive bands, and 100 000 AC-OPF-feasible scenarios are generated (Section 3.2).

• A systematic ablation over all combinations of the four losses (Tables 2 and 3) that reports accuracy against ground truth and fidelity to the teacher separately, since the two are known to diverge [Stanton et al., 2021].

• Edge deployment of the 0.6M-parameter student on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin, with measured latency and memory (Section 4.2).

## 2 State of the Art

## 2.1 Related Work

Learning-based power flow and grid foundation models. The first GNN solver for AC power flow was trained by minimising the Kirchhoff violation directly, without labelled solutions [Donon et al., 2020]; physics-guided and physics-informed variants followed for power flow [Hu et al., 2021], state estimation [Ngo et al., 2024, Cao et al., 2024] and optimal power flow [Nellikkath and Chatzivasileiadis, 2022, Varbella et al., 2024]. Message-passing surrogates such as PowerFlowNet report 4–48× speed-ups over Newton–Raphson [Lin et al., 2024], iterative self-ensembling schemes improve physical consistency [Zhao et al., 2025], and large GNN OPF solvers scale to thousands of buses and N − 1 contingencies [Piloto et al., 2024], though generalisation across topologies remains the open problem [Arowolo and Cremer, 2025]. Foundation-model pretraining is the most recent answer to it [Hamann et al., 2024, Puech et al., 2025]; the closest competitor to our student is MxGPS, a 1.6M-parameter multiplex graph transformer that is small by design and trained jointly on state estimation and PF [Papaioannou et al., 2026]. We take the complementary route: we start from an existing pretrained GridFM, shrink it by distillation, and measure the result on hardware.

Knowledge distillation for graphs, transformers and physics. Distillation descends from model compression [Bucila et al., 2006] and the observation that shallow networks can mimic deep ones [Ba and Caruana, 2014], and wa popularised through soft targets [Hinton et al., 2015], intermediate hints [Romero et al., 2015] and attention transfer [Zagoruyko and Komodakis, 2017]; Gou et al. [2021] survey the field. On graphs, KD has been used to transfer local structure between GNNs [Yang et al., 2020], to train shallow students from deep teachers [Yan et al., 2020], to compare representation-level objectives [Joshi et al., 2024] and, at the extreme, to replace the GNN by an MLP for inference speed [Zhang et al., 2022, Tian et al., 2025]. For transformers, TinyBERT established the multi-loss template of matching embeddings, hidden states, attention matrices and predictions [Jiao et al., 2020], MiniLM showed that distilling only the last-layer self-attention avoids layer-mapping heuristics [Wang et al., 2020], and attention distillation has been adapted to graph transformers [Huang et al., 2024]. Closest to our physics term, KD-PINN distils a large physics-informed solver into a millisecond-latency student using output and PDE-residual losses [Bounja et al., 2025], and PDE foundation models have been distilled into surrogates orders of magnitude smaller [Musekamp et al., 2026]. None of these targets power-grid models, and none matches the distribution of physical residuals between teacher and student.

Efficient inference at the grid edge. Edge intelligence for power systems is an active field [Gooi et al., 2023, Zhou et al., 2019], and the general compression toolbox is mature: integer quantization [Jacob et al., 2018, Nagel et al., 2021], its GNN-specific form that controls aggregation variance [Tailor et al., 2021], latency-budgeted structural pruning [Shen et al., 2022], once-for-all supernets [Cai et al., 2020] and node-adaptive propagation for GNN inference [Gao et al., 2022]. These techniques are orthogonal to distillation and can be stacked on top of it. Measured GNN inference on Jetson-class devices has so far been reported only for generic benchmark graphs [Barillaro et al., 2025], not for grid models, and without physics metrics. Our deployment uses the standard PyTorch Geometric stack [Fey and Lenssen, 2019].

## 2.2 Research Gap

Three ingredients exist separately but have not been combined. Grid foundation models exist, but their reported speed-ups are obtained on data-centre GPUs and no edge deployment has been shown. Distillation for GNNs and transformers is mature, but it ignores physical consistency, and the physics-aware distillation that has appeared targets PDE solvers rather than typed grid graphs with bus-type-dependent masking. Edge benchmarks of GNNs exist, but not for grid models. This paper sits at the intersection: it distils a pretrained GridFM into a half-depth student with an objective that transfers outputs, latent states, attention and the teacher’s physics-residual profile, ablates every combination of these terms on a renewable version of IEEE 118, and reports latency and memory on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin.

## 3 Methodology

Setup. Let $f _ { T }$ denote a frozen, pretrained GridFM teacher and $f _ { S }$ a reduced-parameter student with the same input interface. Both receive a typed grid graph in which the unknown quantities at each bus are masked according to bus type (Section 3.2), and both are trained on the same masked power-flow objective $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { G r a p h K i t } }$ . The student is additionally trained to reproduce the teacher’s outputs, latent states, attention maps and physics-residual profile through the objective in Eq. 1, and is evaluated on accuracy against ground truth, fidelity to the teacher and physics residuals; architectural footprint and edge-deployment considerations are discussed separately. While reducing the number of parameters generally reduces the computational power required to run a model, for a GNS a reduction in the number of GNN layers has the highest impact on reducing the latency from data ingestion to finished inference. Thus, a student is trained using the same stochastic masked-learning paradigm as the teacher GridFM model Hamann et al. [2024]. By using distillation, we are able to leverage the learned internal representations of the teacher model to improve the quality of the student model Hinton et al. [2015], Gou et al. [2021].

To train the reduced-parameter model, we employ a distillation-based learning framework that incorporates multiple complementary distillation objectives. Specifically, we introduce: (i) an output distillation loss that enforces agreement between the student model’s predictions and those of the teacher model Hinton et al. [2015]; (ii) a physicsinformed distillation loss that aligns the student’s residuals with those of the teacher, distinct from the Kirchhoff’s Current Law physics-informed neural network loss already included in the GraphKit objective Hamann et al. [2024], Ngo et al. [2024]; (iii) a latent distillation loss that constrains sparse intermediate representations of the student to match corresponding internal states of the teacher Romero et al. [2015]; and (iv) an attention distillation loss that transfers information encoded in the teacher’s transformer and graph attention maps to the student network Zagoruyko and Komodakis [2017], Vaswani et al. [2017], Velickovic et al. [2018]. It should be noted that the teacher model’s weights are frozen during training, such that backpropagation only occurs within the student model.

These objectives were chosen to maximize the amount of knowledge transferred between the student and teacher. While the GraphKit objective grounds the student model in the given task, the output and physics-residual distillation losses transfer knowledge about the teacher’s prediction and residual structure. The latent and attention losses then transfer information about the internal process used by the teacher to make predictions.

The training loss used during distillation is composed of the GraphKit objective, including supervised training loss and the Kirchhoff’s Current Law physics-informed neural network term, together with the weighted distillation terms described above. This total objective is given in Equation 1.

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { t o t a l } } = \mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { G r a p h K i t } } + \lambda _ { \mathrm { o u t } } \mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { o u t } } + \lambda _ { \mathrm { p h y s } } \mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { p h y s K D } } + \lambda _ { \mathrm { l a t } } \mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { l a t } } + \lambda _ { \mathrm { a t t } } \mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { a t t } } . } \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The output term in Equation 2 resembles the most basic form of distillation Hinton et al. [2015]. Specifically, by training on the outputs of the teacher rather than merely on the objective, a student model integrates information about the accuracy and structure of teacher predictions without overconstraining the training target.

$$
\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { o u t } } = \frac { 1 } { \left| \mathcal { V } _ { b } \right| } \sum _ { i \in \mathcal { V } _ { b } } \left\| \hat { \mathbf { y } } _ { S , i } ^ { b } - \hat { \mathbf { y } } _ { T , i } ^ { b } \right\| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } + \frac { 1 } { \left| \mathcal { V } _ { g } \right| } \sum _ { j \in \mathcal { V } _ { g } } \left\| \hat { \mathbf { y } } _ { S , j } ^ { g } - \hat { \mathbf { y } } _ { T , j } ^ { g } \right\| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } .\tag{2}
$$

Similarly, physics residual distillation allows the student model to better integrate information about which physical residuals can be cleanly reduced by imitating the teacher’s physical residual profile. In this sense, the teacher provides a smoother target for the reduced-capacity student than the physics residual alone. A direct physics residual penalty may force the student to spend excessive capacity on poorly constrained values, such as end-node voltages with limited information, while residual distillation can instead guide the student toward the residual patterns that remain meaningful under the teacher’s learned physical representation. The resulting loss is shown in Equation 3.

$$
\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { p h y s K D } } = \frac { 1 } { \vert \mathcal { M } \vert } \sum _ { ( \ell _ { S } , \ell _ { T } ) \in \mathcal { M } } \left. \rho _ { S } ^ { ( \ell _ { S } ) } - \rho _ { T } ^ { ( \ell _ { T } ) } \right. _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } .\tag{3}
$$

Physics residuals in GridFM are calculated for active and reactive power. In Equation 4, these residuals are summarized as a mean bus-level residual norm for each matched model layer.

$$
\rho _ { M } ^ { ( \ell ) } = \frac { 1 } { | \mathcal { V } _ { b } | } \sum _ { i \in \mathcal { V } _ { b } } \left\| \left[ \begin{array} { l } { r _ { M , i } ^ { P , ( \ell ) } } \\ { r _ { M , i } ^ { Q , ( \ell ) } } \end{array} \right] \right\| _ { 2 } , \qquad M \in \{ S , T \} .\tag{4}
$$

Latent distillation loss is introduced to allow the student to imitate the intermediate representations of the teacher model, following the general idea of using intermediate hints to train thinner networks Romero et al. [2015]. Since the student model has fewer layers, an experiment-specific assignment of student layers to teacher layers must be defined. The latent-state matching objective is given in Equation 5.

$$
\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { l a t } } = \frac { 1 } { \left| \mathcal { M } \right| \left| \mathcal { T } \right| } \sum _ { ( \ell _ { S } , \ell _ { T } ) \in \mathcal { M } } \sum _ { \tau \in \mathcal { T } } \frac { 1 } { \left| \mathcal { V } _ { \tau } \right| } \sum _ { i \in \mathcal { V } _ { \tau } } \left\| P _ { \tau } ^ { ( \ell _ { S } , \ell _ { T } ) } \mathbf { h } _ { S , i } ^ { \tau , ( \ell _ { S } ) } - \mathbf { h } _ { T , i } ^ { \tau , ( \ell _ { T } ) } \right\| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } .\tag{5}
$$

The final type of distillation loss implemented, shown in Equation 6, is the attention loss, following the general principle of attention transfer Zagoruyko and Komodakis [2017]. This term encourages the attention maps of the

student model to match the attention maps of the teacher model. In this way, the student learns from the teacher what data it must attend to. Since the student model typically has fewer heads, only averaged attention maps are compared, such that attention may be redistributed among heads by the student.

$$
\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { a t t } } = \frac { 1 } { | \mathcal { M } | | \mathcal { R } | } \sum _ { ( \ell _ { S } , \ell _ { T } ) \in \mathcal { M } } \sum _ { r \in \mathcal { R } } \frac { 1 } { | \mathcal { E } _ { r } | } \sum _ { e \in \mathcal { E } _ { r } } \left. \bar { \alpha } _ { S , e } ^ { r , ( \ell _ { S } ) } - \bar { \alpha } _ { T , e } ^ { r , ( \ell _ { T } ) } \right. _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } .\tag{6}
$$

## 3.2 Domain

Distillation is applied here to a power system domain foundation model, as described in Section 3. The monitoring task it performs is load flow, or power flow (PF), the most basic study of an power grid network: given how generation and load balance across the network, PF determines the voltage magnitude and the phase angle at every node, and the flow on a branch follows from those two quantities at its two end nodes together with the branch admittance. Each node carries four quantities, the active power $P _ { g }$ and the reactive power $Q _ { g }$ injected by generation there, and the voltage magnitude $V _ { m }$ and phase angle $V _ { a } .$ , with the demand $( P _ { d } , Q _ { d } )$ specified as an input. What is unknown at a node, and therefore what has to be predicted, depends on its bus type, and this classical convention is exactly what makes PF a masked prediction problem: PQ buses predict the voltage $( V _ { m } , V _ { a } )$ , PV buses predict $( V _ { a } , Q _ { g } )$ , and the slack bus predicts $( P _ { g } , Q _ { g } , V _ { m } )$

Power flow is traditionally solved by numerical methods, most commonly Newton-Raphson (NR), and we use an open-source Julia tool to generate the data and labels for both the foundation model and the distilled model. The experiments run on the IEEE 118-node benchmark grid as provided in the PGLib library IEEE PES Task Force on Benchmarks for Validation of Emerging Power System Algorithms [2017], whose nominal load is 4242 MW / 1438 Mvar. That benchmark is a conventional synchronous system, where synchronous generators manage active and reactive power inherently and, as long as the load stays within the total generation capacity, balance it with few violations. It also leaves the balancing to a single reference node, the slack bus, which takes up whatever the fixed generation fails to cover, and that is where the resulting stress becomes visible.

This case study moves the benchmark towards a renewable grid: the flexibility to meet the additional load demand comes from distributed generation rather than from the slack bus, and with it the active and reactive power and hence the voltage are regularised on the renewable side. The slack bus is retained as the angle reference, but carries only about 7% of the scenario-to-scenario variation in active dispatch against roughly three quarters at the flexible units. Each renewable generator carries a boolean flag, set to 1 when it participates in the flexible dispatch, and that flag releases two resources: a battery for active power, and converter control for reactive power. The flag is redrawn independently for every scenario: a count $k \sim \mathcal { U } \{ 5 , \dots , 1 5 \}$ is sampled and a random k-subset of the 26 renewable buses is activated, so that both how much flexibility is available and where it sits vary across scenarios, while the remaining buses are deactivated $( P _ { g } ^ { \operatorname* { m a x } } = Q _ { g } ^ { \operatorname* { m a x } } = Q _ { g } ^ { \operatorname* { m i n } } = 0 )$ and reverted to PQ so their solved voltage cannot enter the model as a known input. The battery enters the merit order, the order in which resources are dispatched cheapest first, at roughly 18 EUR/MWh. The PGLib coefficients put the cheapest conventional unit at 13 EUR/MWh and the next at 22 EUR/MWh, with the rest of the fleet spread over 24–35 EUR/MWh and the reference unit at 26 EUR/MWh, so the battery is dispatched second, after the cheapest unit and ahead of the whole synchronous fleet. Each of the 26 converted renewable buses is now a dispatchable generator, sized from the synchronous unit that once stood there: it active capacity matches that unit’s rating, and its reactive band is the wider of the unit’s native range and $\pm 0 . 4 4 P _ { g } ^ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ the inverter reactive requirement of IEEE Std 1547-2018 IEEE [2018].

Accuracy against ground truth. Every distilled configuration improves on the baseline student for every predicted quantity, so the transfer is never harmful in aggregate. The size of the improvement, however, differs sharply by quantity. Taking the best configuration for each, distillation removes 47.9% of the baseline error on active-power dispatch $P _ { g } ,$ 30.4% on the voltage angle $V _ { a } ,$ , 29.4% on the voltage magnitude $V _ { m }$ and 28.6% on reactive-power dispatch $Q _ { g } .$ . A more informative measure is how much of the gap between the baseline student and the teacher is recovered: 74.6% for $V _ { m } , 6 0 . 6 \%$ for $P _ { g }$ and 41.9% for $V _ { a }$ . In absolute terms the best student remains 1.17× the teacher error on $V _ { m }$ but 2.5× on both $V _ { a }$ and $P _ { g } ,$ so halving the message-passing depth costs most in the angle and dispatch channels, which are the ones that depend on propagating information across the network rather than on local nodal quantities.

Output distillation combined with latent distillation is the best configuration for $V _ { m } , V _ { a }$ and $P _ { g } .$ . The exception is $Q _ { g } ,$ whose best configuration additionally includes $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { a t t } }$ . We report this rather than interpret it: the $Q _ { g }$ column spans only 33.746 to 35.240 across the eight distilled configurations, a range narrow enough that the ordering within it should not carry an architectural conclusion on a single seed.

Reactive power falls below the teacher. All eight distilled configurations achieve a lower $Q _ { g }$ error than the teacher (33.746–35.240 against 35.692), while the undistilled baseline is far worse at 47.236; the effect is therefore attributable to distillation rather than to the reduced architecture. A student surpassing its teacher on a subset of outputs is a documented outcome of distillation [Stanton et al., 2021, Beyer et al., 2022]: the teacher’s predictions are a smoother regression target than the AC-OPF labels, and a reduced-capacity model fitted to that target can generalise better on the noisiest channel. $Q _ { g }$ is the plausible candidate, being the least constrained quantity in the case study, set by converter control within the wide IEEE 1547 band rather than pinned by the power-flow equations. Isolating the mechanism would require varying student capacity, which we have not done.

Fidelity to the teacher selects different objectives. The configuration that best matches ground truth is not in general the one that best imitates the teacher. The two agree for $V _ { m } .$ , where output plus latent distillation minimises both, and disagree for dispatch: the lowest teacher-referenced error for $P _ { g }$ comes from output distillation alone and for $Q _ { g }$ from output plus attention distillation, neither of which is the most accurate configuration for that quantity. Relative to the baseline, the best configurations reduce teacher-referenced error by 57.4% on $V _ { m } , 5 6 . 2 \%$ on $P _ { g }$ and 80.8% on $Q _ { g } ,$ , all larger than the corresponding gains against ground truth. The added terms therefore buy fidelity more readily than accuracy, and the two should be reported separately, as Stanton et al. [2021] argue for classification.

## 4.1 Discussion

Output matching carries the transfer. Matching the teacher’s outputs recovers most of what is recoverable, and the three terms that reach into its internal computation add little. This is consistent with systematic comparisons of representation-level objectives on graphs [Joshi et al., 2024]: a PF teacher’s prediction is already a dense, per-node, physically typed signal, unlike the single soft label of a classification task, so the marginal information in its hidden states is smaller. For this class of surrogate the simplest objective is therefore most of the method, and the cheapest, needing no layer mapping, no projection matrices and no access to teacher internals.

The physics-residual term is harmful in its aggregated form. The term we introduced as the novel component of the objective is the one that consistently degrades accuracy, and the formulation in Eq. 4 is, we believe, the reason. It reduces the entire bus-level residual field to one scalar per layer, the mean norm over buses, before matching. Two residual patterns with the same mean but different spatial distributions are indistinguishable under this target, so the term carries almost no information about where the teacher’s physical error sits, which was the property it was meant to transfer. What it does carry is a magnitude, and matching a magnitude is wrong in a specific way: wherever the teacher’s residual exceeds the student’s, the term pushes the student to become less physically consistent in order to agree, working against the absolute Kirchhoff penalty already inside $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { G r a p h K i t } }$ . The layer mapping compounds this, since residuals at an intermediate layer of a 12-layer teacher and at the matched layer of a 6-layer student are at different stages of refinement.

The term therefore seems worth reformulating rather than discarding. Matching the residual vector at each bus, tailweighted so that a few weak or heavily loaded buses cannot be averaged away, would preserve the spatial information the present form discards; making the penalty one-sided, active only where the student’s residual exceeds the teacher’s, would remove the incentive to inherit teacher error. We state these as the natural next experiment rather than as a result.

What these experiments do not establish. Two limitations bound every claim above. All configurations use a single seed, so the ± values in Table 3 describe spread across configurations rather than run-to-run variation, and the smaller effects, particularly the attention entries and the ordering within the $Q _ { g }$ column, need replication. More importantly for a paper whose central term is physical, we report no physics-residual metric, so it remains possible that physics-residual distillation improves nodal power balance while costing RMSE, a trade an operator might accept; adding mean, p95 and maximum nodal residual per configuration would most strengthen the study.

## 4.2 Edge-Deployment Considerations

The parameter reduction in Table 1 is deterministic: the student has 32.5× fewer parameters, with FP32 weight storage falling from 76.6 MiB to 2.35 MiB, and reduces the message-passing depth from 12 layers to 6. These figures establish a substantially smaller architectural footprint, but they do not by themselves establish end-to-end latency or peak runtime memory on an edge accelerator. In GNN inference at batch size one, irregular gather/scatter operations and the number of message-passing rounds can dominate arithmetic on weights [Barillaro et al., 2025]; activations, graph buffers and runtime workspaces also contribute to peak memory. Device-level benchmarking under a fixed power mode, synchronized timing and a defined warm-up protocol is therefore left to a subsequent revision rather than inferred from parameter count alone.

## 5 Conclusions

We asked whether a grid foundation model can be reduced to a size appropriate for edge inference without losing the behaviour that makes it useful, and which parts of the teacher are worth transferring. Distilling a 12-layer, 20.1M parameter GridFM into a 6-layer, 0.62M-parameter student on a renewable variant of IEEE 118 recovers much of the capacity lost to the reduction: against the same architecture trained without a teacher, error falls by up to 48%, the best configuration closes three quarters of the student-teacher gap on voltage magnitude, and on reactive-power dispatch every distilled student is more accurate than the teacher it was distilled from.

The decomposition of the objective is the more useful result, and it is largely negative. Output matching accounts for almost all of the transfer; latent-state matching adds a small reactive-power improvement; attention matching adds nothing separable from run-to-run variation; and physics-residual distillation, the term we proposed as the contribution of the objective, degrades accuracy on all four quantities. We attribute this to the aggregated form in which we posed it, which collapses a bus-level residual field to one scalar per layer and so transfers a magnitude rather than a spatial pattern, while giving the student an incentive to reproduce the teacher’s physical error rather than reduce it. A buslevel, tail-weighted and one-sided reformulation would test the formulation rather than the underlying idea, and is the obvious next step. That accuracy and fidelity select different configurations is a further reason to report the two separately here rather than treat teacher agreement as a proxy for quality.

Two limits bound these conclusions: a single seed, so the smaller effects need replication, and the absence of a physics-residual metric, without which we cannot say whether the physics term trades accuracy for physical consistency, which for grid operation would be a different verdict on it. The 32.5× parameter reduction and the halved depth are properties of the architecture, and we have kept them separate from any latency claim, which for a GNN at batch size one is governed by memory access rather than weight count and must be established on the device (Section 4.2). Beyond that, the natural continuations are the reformulation above, stacking quantization and hardware-aware pruning, which are orthogonal to distillation [Tailor et al., 2021, Shen et al., 2022], adaptive inference depth [Rahmath P et al., 2024], and moving from state estimation to control, which would require a safety mechanism and closed-loop evaluation.
